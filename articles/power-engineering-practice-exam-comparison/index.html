Power Engineering Practice Exams Compared

Everything sold to power engineering candidates gets called a practice exam. Five quite different things are hiding under that name, and picking the wrong one costs you a rewrite, not just a subscription.

This is a comparison of categories, not brands. Providers change their prices, their question counts and their features, and any article listing today's numbers is wrong within a year. What doesn't change is the shape of the options and the handful of checks that predict whether one will actually get you across 65%.

The five things people call "practice exams"

1. Past papers

Real questions from real sittings. Nothing else tells you as honestly what the examiner's language looks like.

Good for: calibrating to the real thing, late in your preparation. Falls short: supply is limited, and there is no explanation attached, so a past paper measures you without teaching you. Burn them too early and you have nothing left to test yourself with. There is more on how to ration them in our guide to past papers for 2nd Class. Cost: usually free or near-free.

2. Free question sets

Sample questions given away by providers, schools and forums.

Good for: sampling a provider's question quality before paying, and filling odd gaps. Falls short: coverage is deliberately partial, quality is uneven, and some circulating sets are years out of date against the current syllabus. Cost: free, sometimes in exchange for your email.

3. Paid question banks

A large bank of multiple-choice questions organized by chapter or unit, usually with timed exam simulation and unit-level analytics.

Good for: volume and exam pace. If you already know the material, this is the right tool and you should buy one. Falls short: a bank can tell you the answer is B; it cannot teach you the concept behind B. If you never learned the concept, retaking the question just re-teaches you the item. Cost: commonly priced per exam part, monthly or bi-weekly, which means the price multiplies by the number of parts you are studying and by how long you take.

4. Per-paper courses

Course material sold one paper at a time, usually a one-time purchase with lifetime or fixed-term access.

Good for: candidates who need teaching and are working through papers one at a time with long gaps between them. Falls short: the per-paper price is the whole story. Six papers at a per-paper price is a large number, and buying paper five does nothing for you while you are still stuck on paper two. Cost: highest total for anyone completing a full class.

5. All-inclusive platforms

Lessons plus practice for every paper in the class, on a subscription.

Good for: upgraders studying around shift work, anyone studying two papers at once, and anyone whose timeline is measured in years rather than months. Falls short: you pay monthly whether you study that month or not, and if you only need a cheap drill for one paper you are paying for material you won't open. Cost: flat, so the per-paper cost falls the more papers you take.

The structural point: per-paper and per-part pricing multiplies. Flat pricing doesn't. Work out what your choice costs across every paper in the class at your real pace before you compare it to anything else, because the number on the first checkout page is not the number you will pay. The seven checks that actually predict whether it will work

  1. Explanations that teach, not answer keys. "The answer is C" is a scorecard. "The answer is C because the process is adiabatic, so Q equals zero" is teaching. Look at an actual wrong-answer explanation before you buy.
  2. Calculation questions broken into steps. A multi-step problem marked only on the final number tells you that you failed, not where. On papers like 2A1 and 2A2 that difference is the whole game.
  3. Alignment by paper, not by topic. The SOPEEC syllabus is public and organized by paper. Content that maps to 2A2 section by section is verifiable. Content described as "thermodynamics" is not.
  4. Something to do about a weak unit. Analytics that tell you B2 thermodynamics is your weak spot are only useful if there is material behind the finding you can go and study.
  5. Coverage over raw question count. A big bank concentrated in easy chapters is worse than a smaller one that covers every objective. Ask how the questions are distributed, not how many there are.
  6. Offline and mobile. Most real study time happens on shift, on break, or in a truck with no signal.
  7. Total cost at your real pace. Not the monthly figure. The figure across every paper, at the speed you actually move.

How to test question quality in ten minutes

Every serious provider gives you something free. Use it deliberately rather than clicking through it.

  • Answer a calculation question wrong on purpose. Then read what comes back. If it just shows the correct option, you now know what you are buying.
  • Find a definition question. Good SOPEEC-style distractors are all plausible and differ by precision. If the wrong options are obviously wrong, the questions are not exam-realistic and will flatter your score.
  • Look for a question that needs a diagram. Missing or broken graphics are the most common quality failure in this niche, and they cluster in exactly the topics that need them.
  • Check one fact against the syllabus. If a sample question tests something that isn't on the paper's syllabus section, the mapping is loose.
  • Check whether anyone answers you. Send a question in. How a provider responds before you pay is a fair preview of what happens after.

What "SOPEEC-aligned" should mean

It should mean the content maps to the numbered sections of the current reference syllabus for that specific paper. The syllabus is a public document, so this is checkable rather than a claim you have to take on faith.

One thing to be skeptical about: SOPEEC publishes no per-topic question count for any 2nd Class paper. It publishes the format, 3 hours and 100 multiple-choice questions, and the 65% pass mark. So when a provider tells you a paper is "64 questions of applied mechanics" or "about 20 questions on lubrication", that is an estimate presented as a fact. It might be a reasonable estimate. It is not published. Our own paper guides say so and give the syllabus scope instead: 2A1, 2A2, 2A3, 2B1, 2B2 and 2B3.
