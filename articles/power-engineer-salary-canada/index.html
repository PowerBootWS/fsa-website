Power Engineer Salary in Canada

Federal wage data by province, set beside the number of power engineering jobs each province actually posted over three months. The two columns do not tell the same story.

There are two questions behind "what does a power engineer make in Canada", and most articles only answer one of them. The first is what the wage data says. The second is where the jobs actually are, because a high median in a province that posts twelve roles a year is a statistic, not an opportunity.

This page puts both side by side.

What the wage data says

The wage figures below come from Job Bank's wage report for power engineers, the federal government's own labour market data. They were last updated on 19 November 2025, and are drawn from Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey for the 2023 to 2024 reference period. The three territories use a Small Area Estimation model for 2024 instead, because the survey sample is too small.

The posting column is ours: the number of certification-naming power engineering jobs the Full Steam Ahead job board indexed in that province over the three months from 8 May to 8 August 2026.

Reading the table properly

The national median is $49.23/hour. At 2,080 hours that is roughly $102,000 a year before overtime and shift premiums, which for most power engineers is not a rounding error.

Alberta has the highest median in the country at $57.69, about $120,000 annualized. Ontario is a hair behind at $57.00 but has the highest ceiling of anywhere in Canada at $87.00. If you are chasing the top of the range rather than the middle of it, Ontario's nuclear and heavy industrial work is where that number lives.

British Columbia has the highest floor at $35.00 and one of the lower medians at $41.00. That is not a contradiction. BC's advertised demand is heavily weighted to 4th Class institutional work, which pays a solid entry rate and has a shorter ladder above it.

Saskatchewan has one of the widest spreads: a $25.87 low against a $69.71 high, almost matching Alberta's ceiling on a much lower median. Wide spread, thin middle, and which end you land on is largely a certification question.

Prince Edward Island is the outlier at the bottom, with a $28.50 median, below every other jurisdiction and barely above its own $24.92 floor.

Where the money is versus where the work is

Line the two columns up and the map changes shape.

  • Alberta wins on both counts. Highest median in Canada and 480 postings in three months, more than BC and Ontario combined. If the question is purely "where should I go to work as a power engineer", the data does not leave much room for debate.
  • Ontario has the ceiling but not the volume. An $87.00 high end, and 254 postings, roughly half of Alberta's.
  • Manitoba is the quiet one. A $39.50 median, below the national figure, but 100 postings in three months, fourth in the country and well ahead of Saskatchewan.
  • Nova Scotia posts more than Saskatchewan — 69 against 55 — on the second-lowest median in Canada at $35.00.
  • The territories pay well and post almost nothing. Yukon's $49.36 median matches the national figure and Nunavut's floor of $35.43 is the highest in the country, but between them the three territories produced 28 postings in three months.

A caveat that matters more than any single number here. Job Bank reports the occupation as a whole. It does not break wages down by certificate class, so a 5th Class building operator and a 1st Class chief engineer sit inside the same distribution. When you see a province spanning $25 to $70 an hour, a large part of that gap is the distance between the bottom and the top of the certification ladder, not luck or negotiation.

What actually moves your number

Your certificate class. This is the lever you control. It is also the one the wage table hides, for the reason above. Nationally, across the 1,351 postings we indexed, 4th Class was named in 774, 3rd Class in 484, 2nd Class in 193 and 1st Class in 77. Demand thins as you climb, and so does the pool of people who can apply.

Your sector. The largest industry groups in our national posting data were hospitals and health care (78 postings), real estate and facilities management (47), oil and gas (44), education (22), utilities (19) and construction (19). Institutional work is steadier and more urban. Heavy industry is where the upper half of the wage range sits and where the plants often are not.

Your province, but less than you think. Certification is interprovincial. A 2nd Class ticket earned in Saskatchewan is the same ticket in Alberta, which means the wage table is partly a menu rather than a fixed hand.

Hours, not just rate. These are hourly medians. Continuous operations run shift work, and overtime and premiums sit on top of every figure in that table. Two operators on the same posted rate can end a year a long way apart.

How to use these numbers

  1. Compare your own rate to your province's median, not to Canada's. The provincial spread is far too wide for a national figure to tell you anything about your own position.
  2. If you sit below the median, ask which of the two reasons applies: your certificate class, or your sector. Those have different fixes.
  3. Price the upgrade against the gap. If the class above you routinely works at the upper end of your province's range, the arithmetic on getting there is usually short. See what 2nd Class actually costs.
  4. Check the live market before drawing conclusions. Three-month snapshots move. The job board is current and filters by province and class.
