Stationary Engineer vs Power Engineer: What the Job Titles Actually Mean

A posting calls itself "Thermal Operating Technician" and asks for a 3rd Class ticket. Another says "Stationary Engineer" and wants the same thing. Here's what actually separates these titles, and it isn't the job.

If you've spent any time scrolling job boards with a power engineering certificate in hand, you've hit this confusion. One posting says "stationary engineer." Another says "power engineer." A third says neither, and calls the role something you'd never think to search for. All three might be the exact same job, requiring the exact same certificate, at the exact same class of plant.

That's not an accident of bad job posting writing. It's how the trade actually presents itself across Canada. The title on a posting is set by the employer, the sector, and sometimes just local convention. It is not set by your certificate, and it doesn't change what the work is. This article uses our own job board's posting data to show how much the title actually varies, and gives you a practical way to tell whether a posting is a power engineering job regardless of what it calls itself.

The Title Varies. The Certificate Doesn't.

Your certificate class is issued by a provincial regulator against a national SOPEEC standard. It doesn't have a job title attached to it. What gets attached is whatever the hiring employer, HR department, or industry convention decides to call the role that requires it. Across the postings our power engineering job board has indexed, we've seen a wide spread of titles used for roles that require or prefer a power engineering certificate: stationary engineer, plant operator, building engineer, utility operator, shift coordinator, facilities engineer, process operator, and power plant operator.

None of those titles is more "correct" than another. They're sector conventions. A commercial building running a 4th Class ticket on a boiler plant is more likely to post "building engineer" or "facilities engineer." An industrial or utility site running the same certificate requirement is more likely to post "power plant operator" or "stationary engineer." The certificate requirement is the constant. The title is the variable.

The same employer can even use two different titles for two different shifts of the same certificate requirement, depending on who wrote the posting or which HR template got pulled that week. Large organizations with several facilities are especially prone to this, because each site or division tends to post its own openings without checking what the sister site called the equivalent role last year. None of that inconsistency reflects a real difference in the job. It reflects how job postings actually get written, by different people, at different times, with no shared style guide.

Where "Stationary Engineer" Actually Shows Up

We can put a number on how common the straightforward titles actually are, because our job board tracks it by province. In Nova Scotia, 62% of postings say stationary engineer, power engineer, or operating engineer outright, the highest share of any province we've measured. Ontario, by comparison, sits at 41%. Both provinces post real power engineering work in volume. Nova Scotia's employers are simply far more likely to use one of the plain, expected titles when they advertise it.

That gap matters if you're job searching across provinces. A search strategy built entirely around the phrase "stationary engineer" will surface more of what's actually out there in Nova Scotia than it will in Ontario, where a meaningful share of the same work is posted under a title you wouldn't think to search for. What the Messy Titles Actually Look Like

Percentages only tell you so much. Here's what the other 38% to 59% of postings, the ones that don't use an expected title, actually look like in practice. These are real posting titles our job board has captured for roles built around a power engineering certificate:

  • Thermal Operating Technician
  • Utility Operations Manager
  • Process Operator
  • Supervisor Utilities and Yard Operations
  • Production Supervisor 2nd Class TSSA
  • Powerhouse Stationary Engineer/Operator
  • Assistant Shift Engineer

Look at that list and the pattern is obvious once you see it. "Thermal Operating Technician" and "Utility Operations Manager" give you no hint from the title alone that a certificate is involved at all. "Production Supervisor 2nd Class TSSA" buries the requirement in the middle of the title, almost as an afterthought. "Powerhouse Stationary Engineer/Operator" hedges between two titles in the same string, as if the employer itself wasn't sure which convention to use.

If you were searching by job title alone, most of that list would never show up in your results. That's the actual risk here: not that the jobs don't exist, but that title-based searching misses a large share of them.

The signal isn't the title. It's the certificate class named in the requirements and the plant description in the posting body. A posting asking for a 3rd Class ticket to run a thermal plant is a power engineering job, whatever it calls the role.

How to Actually Judge a Posting

Stop screening postings by title and start screening them by two things: the certificate class named in the requirements, and the plant or facility description in the body of the posting. Those two details tell you whether a role is actually power engineering work, and whether your certificate qualifies you for it, regardless of whatever the title says at the top.

In practice, that means reading past the job title on every posting that mentions a plant, a boiler, a powerhouse, a utility system, or an industrial process, even when the title itself gives you no reason to click. A "Utility Operations Manager" posting that names a 2nd Class certificate and describes a central plant is exactly as relevant to you as one titled "2nd Class Power Engineer." The title got you nothing either way. The requirements section is where the actual answer lives.

Provincial volume matters here too, because it tells you where to spend your search effort. Over a recent three-month window, our job board recorded roughly 480 postings in Alberta, 269 in British Columbia, 254 in Ontario, 100 in Manitoba, 69 in Nova Scotia, and 55 in Saskatchewan. If you're searching by title alone in a lower-title-clarity market, you're most exposed to missing real openings in the provinces with the most postings to search through in the first place. The Resume Implication: Mirror the Posting

Once you've identified a posting as real power engineering work regardless of its title, the same logic applies when you apply to it. Mirror the employer's own title language in your resume and professional profile, not your own preferred term for the role. If the posting says "Stationary Engineer," use that phrase in your summary alongside your certificate: something like "2nd Class certified power engineer with stationary engineer experience in industrial plants." This isn't about changing what you did. It's about matching the language an applicant tracking system and a hiring manager are both scanning for.

This is the same advice this site already gives on resumes generally: use the posting's own phrasing rather than your own default vocabulary. If you want the fuller version of that guidance, our resume tips for power engineers covers how to structure the rest of the document around it.

The same applies once you're through to an interview. If the employer has been calling the role "Thermal Operating Technician" through the whole process, don't correct them to "power engineer" mid-conversation. Match their language and let your certificate do the talking. Our guide to power engineering interview questions covers what to expect once you're in the room.

It's worth building a wider set of search terms into your own job hunt for the same reason. If you only ever search "power engineer" or "stationary engineer," you're relying on whichever employers happened to use the plain version. Add the certificate class itself, "2nd Class" or "3rd Class," alongside plant and sector terms like "utility operator," "process operator," or "plant operator." That combination catches postings the straightforward title search misses entirely, and it costs you nothing but a few extra minutes running the search.

Summary

Stationary engineer, power engineer, plant operator, building engineer: these are largely the same job, and the title is set by sector and employer convention, not by your certificate or the work itself. Nova Scotia's postings use a plain title 62% of the time, the highest of any province measured, against 41% in Ontario, which means the same title-based search strategy performs very differently depending on where you're looking. Judge a posting by the certificate class it names and the plant it describes, not by what it's called at the top. Then mirror that same title language back in your own application.
