Two Weeks Out: What To Actually Do Before Your Exam

You have two weeks. You are not going to learn the syllabus in two weeks, and anybody telling you otherwise is selling something. What you can still change is how you write the exam: your pace, your ability to see how a question was built, and whether you walk out having left marks on the table that you had already earned. That is what the next fourteen days are for. About an hour a day. No cramming.

What You Are Walking Into

Every SOPEEC paper is the same shape, and knowing that shape cold is worth more in the last two weeks than another pass through a textbook.

Some jurisdictions give you three and a half hours. Plan every one of the next fourteen days around three. If you get the extra half hour on the day, it is a cushion you did not need, and walking in knowing you can finish inside three is worth more than the thirty minutes ever will be.

The Only Numbers You Need To Memorize

Two sets. The first tells you whether you are drowning. The second is how you actually work the paper.

1. The guardrail

Straight-line pace. Four checkpoints, and you should know them without thinking.

These are not your target. If you hit them exactly, you finish at the bell with nothing checked and no time to come back to anything. They are your drowning check: if question 25 arrives and the clock says 1:05, you already know you have to change something, and you know it at the forty-five minute mark instead of at two and a half hours when it is too late to fix.

2. The three passes

This is the target, and it is how you buy yourself twenty-five minutes of review out of the same three hours.

  • 0:00–1:45Pass one. Start to finish, every question, in order. Answer only what you know cold. Anything that needs real thinking gets flagged and you move on immediately. If a question holds you longer than ninety seconds, that is your signal to flag it, not a reason to dig in. You will see roughly two thirds of the paper fall out in this pass.
  • 1:45–2:35Pass two. Work your flagged questions. Do the ones closest to an answer first, not the hardest first. Momentum matters here, and the last thing you want is to burn twelve minutes on your worst question and then rush six that you actually knew.
  • 2:35–3:00Pass three. Stop working and start checking. Confirm every single question has an answer marked. Confirm your answer sheet has not slipped a row. Give your flagged questions one look, and one only.
Never leave a blank. There is no penalty for a wrong answer and a blank is a guaranteed zero. If pass three finds an empty line and you have nothing, eliminate what you can and pick one.
Days 14 – 11

Find out where you actually stand

You cannot plan two weeks around a guess. Four days to replace the guess with a list.

14Days out

Sit a full paper, properly

Three hours, timed, one sitting. No notes, no phone, no getting up. Pick the paper you are least sure about out of 2A1 through 2B3.

This is the most useful three hours of the next fortnight and almost nobody does it, because finding out is uncomfortable. Do it anyway. Everything else on this page depends on the result.

13Days out

Score it by topic, not by total

Your total tells you nothing you can act on. "68 percent" is not a plan. Go through it question by question and sort your wrong answers into topics, then write down the three topics that cost you the most marks.

That list of three is your entire study plan for the next two days. Everything not on it is off the table, including the topics you enjoy and the ones you feel like you should review.

12Days out

Work topic one

One topic, one session. Not a chapter reread. Pull questions on it, get them wrong, and find out specifically why you got them wrong. Rereading feels like studying and produces almost nothing this late.

11Days out

Work topics two and three

Days 10 – 4

Build the skill you are actually tested on

Seven identical days. This block is the whole point of the plan, and it is the part people skip because it does not feel like studying.

Days 3 – 1

Taper

The work is done. These three days exist to make sure you do not undo it.

3Days out

Full timed paper, same conditions as day 14

Three hours, no notes, no phone. Run the three-pass system exactly the way you will on the day.

Then put this result beside your day 14 result. That gap is your evidence, and for most people it is bigger than they expected. Confidence built on a number you produced yourself holds up on exam morning. Confidence built on telling yourself it will be fine does not.

2Days out

Logistics only. No questions.

Confirm the location and how long the trip actually takes at that hour. Confirm what is allowed in the room rather than assuming, because calculator and code book rules vary by jurisdiction and finding out at the door is a bad morning.

  • Photo ID and your exam confirmation
  • Approved calculator, fresh batteries, and a spare if you have one
  • Pens, pencils, eraser
  • Code books tabbed, if your exam allows them in
  • Water and something to eat
  • Route confirmed, alarm set, backup alarm set

▸ What exam day actually looks like

1Day out

Do not study

Read your formula sheet once in the morning if it settles you, then put it away. That is the whole day's studying.

A tired brain reading a chapter for the eleventh time does not add marks tomorrow, it subtracts them. Eat properly. Sleep. This day is not wasted, it is the last piece of preparation and it is the one people are worst at.

▸ Handling the nerves

Exam Morning

Nothing new here. Just execution.

  • Eat something. Three hours is long enough for an empty stomach to cost you marks.
  • Arrive early enough that traffic cannot beat you. Sitting in the parking lot for twenty minutes is fine. Arriving flustered is not.
  • The second you are allowed to write, put your checkpoints on the scrap sheet: 25 at 0:45, 50 at 1:30, 75 at 2:15.
  • Start pass one. Do not read every question twice. Answer what you know, flag what you do not, keep moving.
  • Anything past ninety seconds on pass one gets flagged. It will be easier the second time and it costs you nothing to come back.
  • At 1:45 you should be at the end of the paper. At 2:35 you stop working and start checking, whether you feel ready to or not.
  • Never leave a blank.

The Six Ways A Question Tries To Beat You

These are not tricks and the examiners are not being unfair. Distractors are written deliberately, from the mistakes real candidates make, and once you can name what a question is doing you stop falling for it. One of these per day through days 10 to 4.

1. Qualifier words

Always, never, all, only, must make an option absolute. In plant engineering, absolutes are usually wrong: there is nearly always a condition or an exception, and the option that admits none of them is usually the one built to be discarded.

Generally, most, primarily, normally are softer, and softer options survive scrutiny more often. Read the qualifier before you read the rest of the option, because sometimes it is the only thing separating two answers.

2. EXCEPT and NOT

The question is asking you for the wrong one. Three of your four options will be perfectly true statements, and if you read on autopilot you will pick a true one and feel good about it.

Circle the word EXCEPT or NOT on the paper the moment you see it. Then answer the question that was actually asked. Under time pressure, on question 78, this is where reading speed turns into lost marks.

3. True, but not the best answer

Two options are both correct statements. Only one of them answers the question that was asked. This one catches strong candidates more often than weak ones, because knowing more makes both options look defensible.

When you are torn between two, reread the stem, not the options. The stem is what decides it. Ask what specifically is being asked: the cause, the first action, the primary purpose, the code requirement. The best answer is the one that matches that, not the one that is most impressive.

4. Units and conversions

kPa against psi, kW against kJ/h, gauge against absolute, mass against weight. The wrong answers in a calculation question are not random numbers. They are the results you get from the specific conversion slips candidates actually make, which is exactly why a wrong answer can look so reassuring when you find it in the list.

Write your units at every step and cancel them. If your answer is sitting right there in the options, that is not confirmation. Check the conversion anyway.

5. Regulation versus technical

Some questions ask what the code requires, not what is technically sound. Those are different questions and occasionally they have different answers.

If the stem names an act, a regulation, a code or a standard, answer with what the document says even when you can think of better engineering. If it does not, answer with the engineering. Deciding which kind of question you are looking at, before you read the options, is most of the work.

6. Changing your first answer

Change an answer only when you can name the specific thing you misread: a qualifier you skipped, a NOT you missed, a unit you dropped. That is a real reason and you should absolutely change it.

"It feels wrong now" is not a reason. It is anxiety, it shows up in the last twenty minutes, and it is how candidates who were passing talk themselves into failing. If you cannot say what you got wrong the first time, leave it alone.
